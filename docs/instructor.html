At a glance

How it runs: live coding in Google Colab. For each section: a short slide, then Together (you live-code, attendees type along in their own copy of the notebook), then Your turn (an exercise on their own), then Answer (you show it). Section 5 is a discussion with no new code. The steps for each part are in the speaker notes of its slide (press S in the deck). The notes have all the code and every answer.

By the end, attendees can:

  1. Load location data into a GeoDataFrame, and describe how it differs from a pandas DataFrame.
  2. Explain what a coordinate reference system (CRS) is, and check and change the CRS of a dataset.
  3. Make a static map with a background map, and an interactive map.
  4. Join datasets by location, for example to find the nearest water pump to each address.
  5. Divide an area into catchments around points, and summarise data within each one.
  6. Describe what a map can and cannot tell us, using Snow’s map as an example.

Before you start

  • Run uv run python tests/check_notes.py (see the README). It runs the code in the notes and checks the numbers above.
  • In a fresh Colab session, within a week of the course, run the notes’ code from top to bottom, and check that the maps appear. Check gpd.__version__: the notes need 1.0 or later, for voronoi_polygons().
  • Check that johnsnow.rtwilson.com/data/SnowGIS_SHP.zip still downloads.
  • Have a backup copy of SnowGIS_SHP.zip in your own Google Drive, in case the site is down on the day. Only share it with attendees if the site is down, as the data has no stated licence.
  • Have the home page link ready to paste in the chat.
  • Open the slides and the notes in separate tabs.

Ground rules

  • Type along slowly. Attendees are new to geospatial, and some are new to pandas. Say each line out loud as you type it.
  • Never debug live for long. If something fails, say so, paste the working code from the notes, and move on.
  • The course is online: every Your turn is done on their own, and answers go in the chat.
  • Wait for a thumbs up in the chat after setup and after each Your turn.
  • If running late, skip the optional extra (Snow’s map) and the Extra steps in the exercises first, then shorten the section 5 discussion, not a coding section.

Introduction and setup (15 min)

Together: open the notebook from the home page, save a copy in Drive, run the two setup cells.

Checkpoints: by 2 min the mission and how today works; by 5 the story; by 15 everyone has the data.

Key messages

  • We will recreate a famous piece of public health detective work.
  • Colab runs in the browser. Nothing to install.

Don’t forget

  • Keep the story to 3 minutes. The rest of it comes in section 5.
  • Show the folder icon in Colab, and the SnowGIS_SHP folder.

Watch out for: Colab’s “This notebook was not authored by Google” warning: click Run anyway. People who run the second cell before the first. If the download fails for everyone, share the backup copy and have them upload it with the folder icon.

1. Location data (30 min)

Together: read both files; head(), type(), len(), Count.sum(), value_counts(); add pump_id; deaths.plot(); .crs; set_crs(27700, allow_override=True); to_crs(4326); distance in both CRSs.

Your turn: Where is pump 0? Answer: about 51.5133, -0.1367, Broadwick Street (Broad Street in 1854).

Checkpoints: by 10 min the data is loaded and explored; by 20 CRS done; by 25 Your turn; finish at 30.

Key messages

  • A GeoDataFrame is a DataFrame with a geometry column. All of pandas still works.
  • A CRS says what the numbers mean. set_crs() labels, to_crs() converts.
  • Measure distances in metres (British National Grid), not degrees.

Don’t forget

  • Ask “what are these numbers?” before showing .crs.
  • Show the warning geopandas gives when measuring in degrees.
  • Point out that POINT (x y) puts longitude first.

Watch out for: latitude and longitude swapped in the web map (they land in the Indian Ocean). People expecting the pumps to have names: the file has none, which is why we add pump_id.

2. Making maps (35 min)

Together: build the static map a step at a time (deaths, size by Count, pumps with ax=ax), add a background map with contextily, then explore() with two layers.

Your turn: An interactive map. Answer: the address with 15 deaths is about 17 m from the Broad Street pump.

Checkpoints: by 12 min the layered map; by 18 the background map; by 25 explore(); by 32 Your turn; finish at 35.

Key messages

  • Maps are built in layers. ax=ax draws on the same map.
  • Size and colour can show values from the table.
  • .plot() for static maps, .explore() for interactive ones.
  • Keep the map attribution.

Don’t forget

  • Compare the sized dots to Snow’s stacked bars.
  • If time, show Snow’s original map as the background (the optional extra in the notes). It is a good “wow” moment.

Watch out for: a background map in the wrong place, usually because crs=deaths.crs is missing. People trying other cx.providers: OpenStreetMap’s own tiles return “Access blocked” images to contextily, and CartoDB now needs an API key.

Break (10 min)

About 1 hour 20 minutes in. Colab can disconnect if left idle. If it does, attendees click Runtime → Run before in the first cell of section 3, or rerun the notebook from the top.

3. The nearest pump (35 min)

Together: sjoin_nearest(pumps, distance_col="distance"); explain the new columns; groupby("pump_id")["Count"].sum(); percentages; distance.describe(); map coloured by pump_id with categorical=True.

Your turn: Deaths within 100 metres. Answer: 196 deaths, 40%.

Checkpoints: by 10 min the join; by 20 deaths per pump; by 25 the map; by 32 Your turn; finish at 35.

Key messages

  • A spatial join matches rows by location.
  • After the join, it is a normal table: use pandas.
  • 340 of 489 deaths (70%) were nearest the Broad Street pump.
  • Straight-line distance is not walking distance (back to this in section 5).

Don’t forget

  • Explain groupby() slowly: some attendees will not have used pandas.
  • Point out that pumps 3 and 6 are missing from the groupby result, not zero.

Watch out for: index_right confusing people: it is the row of the pump, added by every spatial join. In the exercise, using < or <= gives the same answer here.

4. Pump catchments (25 min)

Together: the study area box, voronoi_polygons(), label with sjoin(predicate="contains"), clip(), plot; count deaths with sjoin().

Your turn: A map of catchments. Answer: pumps 3 and 6 had no deaths.

Checkpoints: by 10 min the catchments; by 15 deaths per catchment; by 22 Your turn; finish at 25.

Key messages

  • Each Voronoi polygon is the area nearest one pump.
  • The polygons are not in the order of the pumps, so a spatial join labels them.
  • The totals match section 3, by definition. The polygons show the areas.
  • A map shaded by a value per area is a choropleth.

Don’t forget

  • Ask “why are the totals the same?” and wait for answers in the chat.
  • Mention that the same method is used today, for example for the nearest GP surgery.

Watch out for: an AttributeError for voronoi_polygons: the geopandas version is older than 1.0 (see Before you start). Running the exercise’s catchments["deaths"] line before the totals line.

5. What the map cannot tell us (20 min)

Together: the exceptions (straight lines, the brewery, the Hampstead widow, the first case), then the limits of the data.

Your turn: Question the map, in the chat.

Checkpoints: by 7 min the exceptions; by 12 the limits; by 17 Your turn; finish at 20.

Key messages

  • A map shows where deaths were recorded, not why they happened.
  • Snow’s case was persuasive because the map fitted with other evidence.
  • The outbreak was already slowing when the handle was removed.
  • Ask who and what is missing from any map.

Don’t forget

  • Draw out the point about rates: without the population at each address, a crowded street looks worse.
  • Link to today: health data is released for areas, not addresses, to protect privacy, and the boundaries change the pattern.

If running late: take one or two answers from the chat, not more.

Summary (10 min)

  • Go through the summary table on the slides.
  • Go back to the six objectives and tick them off.
  • Point to the next steps, and remind people the notes have everything.
  • Ask people to fill in the feedback form.
